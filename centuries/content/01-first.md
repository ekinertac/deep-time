## Lede

You arrive in the largest city on Earth. It has running water, public baths,
street food, a fire brigade, a police force and a grain dole, and it will
process you within a week.

Rome has an answer for a man who cannot say whose he is, and the answer is not
prison or expulsion. Those are expensive. The answer is that you belong to
somebody, and the only open question is who.

## Clock

Nothing here is trying to kill you. That is the problem.

### Hour 1 | The city works

Paved streets, aqueducts running day and night, bakeries, bars, public
lavatories, a night watch. Nothing you see suggests danger. Everything you have
read about ancient cities being filthy and violent is true and beside the point:
this one functions better than most cities will again for fifteen hundred years.

### Day 1 | You cannot buy anything

You have no coin, and the coin you have never seen would be refused anyway. Food
here is bought, not gathered. There is no forest to walk into and nothing
growing that is not owned. Within a day the entire question of your survival is
the question of who feeds you.

### Day 3 | Somebody asks whose you are

Not out of suspicion. It is the ordinary opening question, the way a stranger
today is asked what they do. You cannot answer it. You have no family here, no
patron, no city of origin anybody has heard of, no manumission tablet and no
one to vouch for you.

A free man travelling has letters. You have a knife and an accent.

### Week 2 | The default assumption

A healthy adult who cannot account for himself is presumed to be running from
somewhere. That presumption is not a hunch, it is close to a legal default, and
there is a trade of professional slave-catchers built on it.

Being wrong about you costs nobody anything. Being right is worth money.

### !Years | Where you end up decides how long you last

This is where the number splits. Sold into a household in the city, you may live
an ordinary length of life, and a literate slave is worth keeping alive and
comfortable: tutors, secretaries and accountants were slaves. Sold to a flour
mill or the Spanish silver mines, you are a consumable, and the arithmetic is
measured in a few years.

The century does not kill you. It assigns you.

## Survive

You will be fed. The terms are the thing.

### Air

Fine. Smoky in winter from a hundred thousand hearths and braziers, and
otherwise unremarkable.

### Water

Better than anything until the twentieth century. Eleven aqueducts, public
fountains running continuously, and enough of it that Rome uses water to move
sewage. Lead pipes, which is worth less worry than it sounds given the mineral
crust that lines them.

### Food

Bought daily and cheap: bread, olives, cheese, fish sauce, watered wine,
lentils. Adequate and monotonous. None of it is free to you, because the free
version goes to a register of citizens.

### !Money

Nothing. You have no coin, no credit, no trade anyone recognises and no guild.
This, not danger, is what closes the century around you.

### Skill

Literacy, and it is genuinely valuable. Perhaps one adult in ten can read, and
demand for scribes, tutors and bookkeepers is real. It is also exactly the skill
that makes you worth buying rather than worth hiring.

### !Standing

None. Not low standing: none. Every route to safety here runs through a person
who agrees to be responsible for you, and acquiring one usually means becoming
theirs.

## Doubt

### Rome's population is an estimate built on grain, not a headcount.

Figures from 500,000 to well over a million are all defended in print. They are
reconstructed from the grain supply, the housing density and the aqueduct
capacity, each of which needs assumptions about consumption. The order of
magnitude is not in dispute; the number is.

### The enslaved share is even softer.

Estimates for Roman Italy run from about 15% to about 35%, and the city itself
is usually put at the higher end. There was no census category that answers this
question directly, so every figure is a reconstruction.

### Whether you would actually be enslaved is reasoning, not evidence.

Roman law on *fugitivi* and the existence of professional slave-catchers are
both well attested, as is the presumption against people who could not account
for themselves. What a specific stranger would have experienced is inference
from that, and should be read as the argument it is.
