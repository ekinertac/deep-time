Century 01 / 20 Imperial peak

First Century

1 – 100 CE · Rome at roughly a million people

You arrive in the largest city on Earth. It has running water, public baths, street food, a fire brigade, a police force and a grain dole, and it will process you within a week.

Rome has an answer for a man who cannot say whose he is, and the answer is not prison or expulsion. Those are expensive. The answer is that you belong to somebody, and the only open question is who.

You last Years, owned

You are unlikely to die quickly here and unlikely to stay free. Rome is fed, policed, watered and lit, and roughly a third of the people in it are property. A healthy adult male with no patron, no papers and an accent nobody can place is not a mystery to this city. He is an asset with a paperwork problem.

Conditions

People in Rome ~1,000,000est.

Scale 0–2.8M; today about 2.8 million. Contested by a factor of two, but no European city will be this size again until London in the 1800s.

Enslaved share of the city ~30%est.

Scale 0–100%. The single most important number on this page, and the reason the verdict reads the way it does.

Life expectancy at birth ~25years

Scale 0–83; today 83 in Italy. Dragged down almost entirely by infant deaths. Reach twenty and your sixties are reachable.

Adults who can read ~10%est.

Scale 0–100%. Your one transferable skill, and it is worth more here than in any later century until the 1400s.

Free bread, if registered ~200,000recipients

Scale 0–1M. The grain dole is real, generous and closed. It goes to a list of citizen names, and adding yourself to it is not a thing that can be done.

Treatment for an infected wound none

Scale 0–100% effective. Honey, wine and vinegar on the wound, which is better than nothing and is not treatment. This line does not change for eighteen more centuries.

What kills you

Nothing here is trying to kill you. That is the problem.

Hour 1

The city works

Paved streets, aqueducts running day and night, bakeries, bars, public lavatories, a night watch. Nothing you see suggests danger. Everything you have read about ancient cities being filthy and violent is true and beside the point: this one functions better than most cities will again for fifteen hundred years.

Day 1

You cannot buy anything

You have no coin, and the coin you have never seen would be refused anyway. Food here is bought, not gathered. There is no forest to walk into and nothing growing that is not owned. Within a day the entire question of your survival is the question of who feeds you.

Day 3

Somebody asks whose you are

Not out of suspicion. It is the ordinary opening question, the way a stranger today is asked what they do. You cannot answer it. You have no family here, no patron, no city of origin anybody has heard of, no manumission tablet and no one to vouch for you.

A free man travelling has letters. You have a knife and an accent.

Week 2

The default assumption

A healthy adult who cannot account for himself is presumed to be running from somewhere. That presumption is not a hunch, it is close to a legal default, and there is a trade of professional slave-catchers built on it.

Being wrong about you costs nobody anything. Being right is worth money.

Years

Where you end up decides how long you last

This is where the number splits. Sold into a household in the city, you may live an ordinary length of life, and a literate slave is worth keeping alive and comfortable: tutors, secretaries and accountants were slaves. Sold to a flour mill or the Spanish silver mines, you are a consumable, and the arithmetic is measured in a few years.

The century does not kill you. It assigns you.

If you survive

You will be fed. The terms are the thing.

Air

Fine. Smoky in winter from a hundred thousand hearths and braziers, and otherwise unremarkable.

Water

Better than anything until the twentieth century. Eleven aqueducts, public fountains running continuously, and enough of it that Rome uses water to move sewage. Lead pipes, which is worth less worry than it sounds given the mineral crust that lines them.

Food

Bought daily and cheap: bread, olives, cheese, fish sauce, watered wine, lentils. Adequate and monotonous. None of it is free to you, because the free version goes to a register of citizens.

Money

Nothing. You have no coin, no credit, no trade anyone recognises and no guild. This, not danger, is what closes the century around you.

Skill

Literacy, and it is genuinely valuable. Perhaps one adult in ten can read, and demand for scribes, tutors and bookkeepers is real. It is also exactly the skill that makes you worth buying rather than worth hiring.

Standing

None. Not low standing: none. Every route to safety here runs through a person who agrees to be responsible for you, and acquiring one usually means becoming theirs.

Where the numbers are soft

Rome's population is an estimate built on grain, not a headcount. Figures from 500,000 to well over a million are all defended in print. They are reconstructed from the grain supply, the housing density and the aqueduct capacity, each of which needs assumptions about consumption. The order of magnitude is not in dispute; the number is.
The enslaved share is even softer. Estimates for Roman Italy run from about 15% to about 35%, and the city itself is usually put at the higher end. There was no census category that answers this question directly, so every figure is a reconstruction.
Whether you would actually be enslaved is reasoning, not evidence. Roman law on fugitivi and the existence of professional slave-catchers are both well attested, as is the presumption against people who could not account for themselves. What a specific stranger would have experienced is inference from that, and should be read as the argument it is.
