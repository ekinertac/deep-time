Era 09 / 16 Paleozoic

Carbon­iferous

358.9 – 298.9 million years ago  ·  60 Myr long  ·  1.3% of Earth's history

The single best era to be dropped into before mammals exist. Oxygen is at its all-time peak, the swamps are full of fuel and fresh water, and nothing on land is big enough to hunt you. The trade is that the whole world is one dropped spark from burning.

You last Years

With basic bushcraft this is survivable indefinitely in principle. What ends it in practice is an infected wound with no antibiotics, or a fire you did not start moving faster than fire moves in air you have ever breathed.

Conditions

Atmospheric oxygen ~30–35%est., peak

Scale 0–40%. The highest in Earth's history and the only era where the air is richer than yours. Expect noticeably better endurance at altitude.

Carbon dioxide ~350ppm, late

Scale 0–2,000 ppm; today ~425 ppm. Forests buried carbon faster than anything could rot it, and drew CO₂ down to near modern levels. That buried carbon is the coal.

Mean global temp ~14 °Clate

Scale −20 to 250 °C. Close to today, but the distribution is not: equatorial swamps stay hot and wet while Gondwana carries continental ice sheets.

Fire risk extreme

Above roughly 25% O₂ even damp vegetation sustains a flame. Charcoal (fusain) is found through the coal record, so fire was near-continuous somewhere.

Day length ~22.4hours

Scale 0–24 h. About 385 days in a year. Short enough to notice if you were counting, not enough to matter.

Largest land animal 2.6 marthropleura

Scale 0–30 m. A millipede longer than a car, and a herbivore. The only land animal here with any interest in hurting you is a 70 cm scorpion.

What kills you

Hour 1

The air is better than yours

At 30%+ oxygen, moderate exertion costs less. The effect is real but modest, and it does not make you superhuman. Sustained partial pressures above about 0.5 bar damage lungs; this sits under that, at roughly 0.3.

Day 1

Your campfire escapes

This is the era's actual signature threat. Ignition is easy, flame spread is fast, and material you would consider too wet to burn will carry fire. The habit you built in normal air, that a fire needs tending to stay alive, is exactly backwards here.

Week 1

You find out what you can digest

The forest is enormous and almost entirely inedible: lycopsid bark, lignin, cellulose. Your calories come from fresh water instead. Fish, amphibians, freshwater arthropods, and seed ferns, which do produce real seeds.

Month 1–6

A cut goes bad

No pathogen here has evolved to target a mammal, so you will not catch a disease. Environmental bacteria in a wound do not care. This is the most likely thing to actually kill you, in every era from here to the Pleistocene.

Year 1+

Or nothing does

Water, fuel, shelter material, protein and a stable climate band all exist within walking distance of each other. Stay in the equatorial belt, away from the Gondwanan ice, and the Carboniferous will not be what ends you.

If you survive

You probably do. Here is what the planet is offering.

Air

Better than modern. The only atmosphere in the deep past you could run a marathon in.

Water

Everywhere, and stagnant. Boil it. Not for pathogens, which are not adapted to you, but for the amoebae and the tannin load.

Food

Freshwater fish, amphibians up to two metres, insects at unusual sizes, and seed-fern seeds. No fruit, no grain, no roots worth digging.

Fire

Trivially easy and genuinely dangerous. Build it on open mud, small, downwind of nothing you own.

Shelter

The first era with real timber. Lepidodendron bark comes off in workable sheets and the wood is soft enough to cut with stone.

Medicine

None. Bring antibiotics or accept that a deep cut is a coin flip.

Where the numbers are soft

The 35% oxygen figure is a model result, and it is contested. It comes from geochemical carbon–sulfur models, chiefly Berner's GEOCARBSULF. Other approaches put the peak nearer 25–30%. The direction is not in dispute — Carboniferous air was oxygen-rich — but the exact number should be read as a range, not a measurement.
Insect gigantism probably has more than one cause. High O₂ is the standard explanation, since insects breathe by diffusion through tracheae rather than pumped blood. The absence of flying vertebrate predators for most of the period likely mattered as much.
The fire argument runs the other way too. Some researchers argue oxygen could not have exceeded ~35% precisely because forests would have burned off faster than they grew. That constraint is one of the main reasons the upper estimates are treated with caution.
